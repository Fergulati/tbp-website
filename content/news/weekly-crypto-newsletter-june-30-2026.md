## THIS WEEK IN CRYPTO

The final week of June has delivered a punishing close to what has been the most volatile quarter of the year for digital assets. Bitcoin sank below \$60,000 as cascading liquidations rattled leveraged traders, Ethereum pressed forward with its biggest protocol overhaul since the Merge, regulators continued filling in the details of landmark stablecoin legislation, and a major new stablecoin consortium launched just as Circle took a hit in the market. Here is what you need to know heading into the new month.

---

### 1. Bitcoin's June Collapse: Cascade Risk and What Comes Next

Bitcoin closed the month of June down roughly 20%, pushing its year to date losses to 34% and sending the asset below \$60,000 for the first time since late 2024. The drop was not a slow bleed. It was a liquidation cascade. Years of one directional long positioning had stacked enormous liquidation clusters between \$65,000 and \$60,000, and once price broke through those levels the exchange risk engines closed positions automatically, compounding the move lower.

Analysts at Bitfinex flagged what they called "a sustained move below the \$60,000 put wall" as the trigger for deeper losses toward the \$54,000 to \$56,000 range. The cascade erased approximately \$1.84 billion in leveraged positions within a 24 hour window, with long positions accounting for close to 85% of total losses. The sell pressure was not purely mechanical either. ETF outflows exceeded \$2 billion for the month, and Strategy sold Bitcoin for the first time in four years, both of which shook narrative confidence precisely when technical support needed to hold.

For consulting clients with Bitcoin treasury exposure, the key takeaway is that leverage concentration in derivatives markets creates asymmetric downside risk during macro stress periods. The situation remains one to monitor closely as the market tests historical long term support levels.

---

### 2. Ethereum's Glamsterdam Hard Fork Enters Final Devnet Phase

While Bitcoin captured the bearish headlines, Ethereum developers quietly confirmed that the Glamsterdam hard fork has entered its final devnet phase and is approaching public testnet deployment. Described by core developers as the largest protocol overhaul since the Merge, Glamsterdam bundles upgrades to execution layer efficiency, new RPC methods for improved node management, and changes designed to lower transaction costs on Layer 2 networks.

The timing matters for the broader ecosystem. Ethereum's total staked supply has climbed to 4.88 million ETH, reflecting continued institutional confidence in the network even during the current bear phase. Meanwhile Bitmine, a publicly traded company led by Fundstrat chairman Tom Lee, now holds nearly 5.4 million ETH, representing roughly 4.47% of circulating supply. Lee's \$250,000 long term price target for ETH, tied to AI and tokenization driven demand for blockspace, keeps the institutional narrative alive despite near term price pressure.

The Ethereum Foundation added complexity to the picture this week by announcing a 20% workforce reduction and plans to cut spending by 40% over the coming years. The restructuring is framed as a long term sustainability measure, though it arrives at an awkward moment. Clients building on Ethereum infrastructure should keep an eye on developer ecosystem health as the Glamsterdam testnet launches.

---

### 3. The CLARITY Act Remains Stuck in the Senate

The CLARITY Act, which would establish a comprehensive market structure framework for digital assets beyond stablecoins, continues to stall in the Senate despite clearing the House in July 2025 and advancing through the Senate Banking Committee in May 2026. This week brought fresh complications. President Trump's refusal to sign an unrelated housing bill that included a CBDC ban has injected political uncertainty into the broader crypto legislative calendar, and no Senate floor vote date has been confirmed.

For the crypto industry, the CLARITY Act represents the second half of the legislative package that began with the GENIUS Act. Without it, questions about which digital assets are securities and which are commodities remain unresolved. The SEC and CFTC issued a joint interpretation in March 2026 placing 16 major tokens under CFTC jurisdiction, but that guidance does not carry the force of law. Clients operating token projects or exchanges should continue planning for a mid 2027 regulatory clarity window at the earliest.

### 4. DeFi Goes Institutional: Robinhood, Revolut, and the TVL Story

Decentralized Finance crossed into another milestone this week as two marquee names in retail finance deepened their on chain footprints. Robinhood announced it is using Arbitrum to facilitate tokenized stock trading for European users, bringing regulated equity exposure onto a public blockchain for the first time at that scale. Revolut integrated Uniswap directly into its app, allowing its 50 million plus users to access decentralized swaps and crypto purchases without leaving the Revolut interface.

These moves reflect a broader trend. DeFi's global market size is forecast to reach \$37.27 billion in 2026, and total value locked across protocols is projected to cross \$200 billion as institutional participation in lending, borrowing, and stablecoin settlement accelerates. The DeFi of 2026 looks meaningfully different from its 2021 incarnation. The speculative yield farming era is giving way to institutional grade infrastructure serving real settlement and liquidity needs. For clients evaluating on chain treasury strategies, the liquidity and counterparty quality in DeFi protocols has improved substantially over the past two years.

### 5. Open USD Launches as Circle Faces New Competition

In what may be the most significant competitive development in the stablecoin market this year, a consortium of more than 140 companies unveiled Open USD, a new stablecoin network backed by Stripe, Coinbase, Mastercard, Visa, and BlackRock. The announcement sent Circle's stock down 8% as investors weighed what a coalition of that scale means for USDC's market position.

The launch comes as the stablecoin market sits at a record \$316 billion total market cap, with USD backed stablecoins accounting for 97% of that total. USDT and USDC together still command approximately 84% of stablecoin market share, though that figure has declined from 89% just a year ago as new entrants gain traction. The arrival of Open USD backed by payment networks that already process trillions of dollars annually signals that the stablecoin market is entering a phase of genuine competition rather than a two player duopoly. Clients building payment infrastructure should evaluate Open USD's technical specifications and regulatory standing carefully as they become available.

## DEEP RESEARCH: The GENIUS Act and the Future of Dollar Backed Digital Assets

*A comprehensive look at the landmark stablecoin legislation reshaping the financial system*

---

### Introduction

On July 18, 2025, President Trump signed the Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for US Stablecoins Act into law. It was the first comprehensive piece of US national crypto legislation and it arrived after years of regulatory ambiguity that had left stablecoin issuers, banks, and users operating without clear rules. Nearly a year into the GENIUS Act era, with federal agencies actively writing implementing regulations and the effective date of January 18, 2027 approaching, it is worth examining what this law actually does, who benefits, who faces pressure, and what it means for the future of the dollar in a world where digital payments are becoming the norm.

Think of the GENIUS Act as the zoning laws for a new kind of financial neighborhood. Before it passed, builders (stablecoin issuers) were constructing all kinds of structures on land with no clear rules. Some were solid. Some were dangerous. The GENIUS Act draws the property lines, specifies what materials you must use, and names the inspectors. Now everyone knows what can be built and where.

---

### The Regulatory Framework: What the Law Actually Requires

The GENIUS Act establishes a federal framework for "permitted payment stablecoin issuers," a new legal category that covers both bank and nonbank entities. The core requirements are straightforward in concept though complex in implementation.

Issuers must maintain one to one reserves for every stablecoin issued. Those reserves must consist of what the law defines as low risk liquid assets, specifically US dollars, Federal Reserve notes, and short term Treasuries or similar instruments. Issuers may not rehypothecate those reserves to fund other investments except under narrowly defined circumstances. Third party auditors must attest to reserve composition on a monthly basis. Annual independent audits are required for larger issuers.

The law also prohibits issuers from paying interest or yield directly to stablecoin holders. This single provision has generated more industry controversy than any other element of the legislation. The yield ban is intended to prevent stablecoins from functioning as uninsured bank deposits at scale, but as discussed below it has created a competitive asymmetry that banks and lawmakers are still debating.

Consumer protections are meaningful and legally enforceable for the first time. Holders gain priority claim over creditors against an issuer's reserve assets in the event of insolvency, meaning stablecoin users would be made whole before general creditors in a bankruptcy scenario. Issuers must publish redemption policies in plain language and can only charge fees that are disclosed upfront and capped, with any fee increases requiring seven days advance notice.

The GENIUS Act also permits states to establish their own regulatory regimes for stablecoin issuers, provided those regimes are "substantially similar" to the federal framework. Treasury's initial proposed rulemaking, published in April 2026, laid out principles for what "substantially similar" means, and the interaction between federal and state oversight remains one of the more complex implementation questions facing the industry.

---

### Who Can Issue Stablecoins and Who Regulates Them

The GENIUS Act creates multiple pathways into the stablecoin market, and the regulator assigned to each issuer depends on its structure and affiliation.

Bank subsidiaries of federally chartered or state chartered depository institutions can issue stablecoins under the oversight of their existing primary regulator, whether that is the OCC, the Federal Reserve, or state banking authorities. This pathway is the most natural for large banks that already have compliance infrastructure and regulatory relationships in place.

Nonbank entities, including fintech companies, crypto native firms, and technology platforms, can seek approval from the OCC to operate as federally qualified payment stablecoin issuers. This creates a federal charter analog for nonbanks that is national in scope, meaning an OCC approved nonbank issuer does not need to obtain separate licenses in each state. This pathway is the one that Tether's US competitors and newer entrants are likely to pursue.

The Federal Reserve participates through a Stablecoin Certification Review Committee that evaluates nonbank applications for systemic risk and compliance, providing an additional layer of oversight for issuers that might pose risks to financial stability.

As of June 2026, the OCC published its notice of proposed rulemaking in March and the FDIC published its proposal in April. FinCEN and OFAC issued a joint proposed rule on anti money laundering and sanctions compliance in April as well. Final rules from all three agencies are required by July 18, 2026, with the statutory 18 month effective date running from enactment. The practical compliance window for issuers is tight, and organizations are already building the legal, technical, and reporting infrastructure they will need before the law takes full effect.

---

### The Compliance Burden: What Issuers Must Actually Do

For any entity seeking to issue stablecoins under the GENIUS Act framework, the operational requirements are substantial and ongoing.

Reserve management requires daily tracking of asset composition, segregation of reserve assets from operational funds, and the legal infrastructure to demonstrate one to one backing at any given moment. Monthly attestations require engaging qualified third party accounting or audit firms, and the public disclosure obligation means any reserve management failure becomes immediately visible to the market.

AML and KYC compliance requires the same level of program investment that applies to regulated financial institutions under the Bank Secrecy Act. This includes transaction monitoring systems, customer due diligence protocols, suspicious activity reporting, and OFAC screening. The OCC's proposed reporting forms, published in a subsequent bulletin, include weekly and quarterly filings that give regulators ongoing visibility into issuer operations.

Capital and liquidity requirements beyond the one to one reserve ratio are still being finalized through the rulemaking process. The FDIC's proposed rule for FDIC supervised issuers includes requirements around capital buffers and risk management standards that would apply on top of the basic reserve mandate.

For smaller fintech companies and crypto native startups, this compliance burden is significant and may ultimately consolidate the stablecoin market toward well capitalized incumbents and large bank affiliates. That consolidation dynamic is worth watching for clients considering whether to build stablecoin products directly or partner with established issuers instead.

---

### Bank vs. Nonbank: The Battle Over Who Controls Digital Payments

The most commercially consequential aspect of the GENIUS Act is the competitive dynamic it has created between traditional banks and nonbank entities. And that dynamic is playing out in real time.

Banks entered the GENIUS Act era with important structural advantages: existing regulatory relationships, large balance sheets, established compliance teams, and deep customer trust. The OCC has affirmed that banks may engage in stablecoin activities including custody, reserve holding, and blockchain based payments without prior approval, provided they maintain appropriate risk controls. Several large banks are actively developing stablecoin products for institutional clients. JPMorgan's JPM Coin, which enables real time wholesale payment settlement, represents the leading example of what bank issued stablecoins can do at institutional scale.

The argument from the banking industry is that nonbank issuers operating under the GENIUS Act do not carry the same regulatory burden or capital requirements as banks, creating an uneven competitive field. More than 40 banking associations, led by the American Bankers Association, sent a joint letter to Congress warning about a specific provision: while the GENIUS Act prohibits stablecoin issuers from paying yield directly to holders, it does not explicitly prevent affiliated exchanges or platforms from offering yield bearing programs on those same stablecoins. Lending protocols on Ethereum and Solana currently offer 5% to 8% annual yields on stablecoin deposits, and banks fear deposit flight toward these arrangements.

Nonbank issuers, for their part, are moving aggressively. Circle, PayPal, and Ethena have expanded stablecoin offerings since the law passed. Major technology companies including Apple, Google, and Meta are exploring stablecoin integration into their platforms. Walmart and FIS are developing stablecoin products that could offer embedded payment experiences at consumer scale. Combined with the arrival of the Open USD consortium, the nonbank side of the market is not waiting for the regulatory landscape to fully settle before expanding.

Grant Thornton's National Industry Leader for Blockchain noted that the dominant stablecoins USDT and USDC saw their combined market share drop from 89% to under 84% over the past year as new entrants gain traction, adding that "the fragmentation of the stablecoin market is already underway."

The competitive outcome of this dynamic will shape digital payments for the next decade. Banks that move quickly and leverage their existing infrastructure have a genuine opportunity to capture stablecoin market share and retain deposits. Banks that wait risk watching payment volume migrate to regulated nonbank issuers and technology platforms.

---

### Consumer Impact: What Changes for Everyday Users

For individual users and businesses holding or transacting in stablecoins, the GENIUS Act introduces protections that did not exist before.

The legally enforceable right to redeem at par on demand is the most fundamental change. Before the law passed, a stablecoin holder's ability to get their dollars back depended entirely on the issuer's policies and reserve quality, with no statutory backstop. Now, redemption rights are legally defined and the reserve backing required to honor them is mandated by law.

The yield prohibition is a meaningful trade off for consumers. Dollar backed stablecoins will not pay interest under the GENIUS Act framework, which limits their attractiveness as a savings instrument compared to money market funds or yield bearing accounts. The law essentially positions regulated stablecoins as payment tools rather than investment instruments, a design choice that prioritizes stability and systemic safety over competitive returns.

For cross border users, the impact is overwhelmingly positive. Stablecoin transaction volume reached \$28 trillion in Q1 2026 alone, a 51% increase from the prior quarter, driven in large part by international payments, remittances, and corporate treasury activity. The ability to move dollar denominated value globally at near zero cost and near instant speed is proving transformative for businesses and individuals in markets where traditional payment infrastructure is slow and expensive. IMF research estimates that stablecoin flows reach 7% to 8% of GDP in parts of Latin America and Africa, where demand for stable dollar access is highest.

For the approximately 25 million US households that are unbanked or underbanked, regulated stablecoins accessible via mobile devices represent a potential on ramp to the broader financial system, offering a practical alternative to check cashing services and high fee prepaid cards.

---

### The Dollar Dominance Dimension

Perhaps the most strategically important aspect of the GENIUS Act is what it does for the US dollar's position in global finance.

As of late 2025, USD backed stablecoins accounted for 99% of all stablecoins in circulation worldwide. The total stablecoin market capitalization of \$316 billion is entirely denominated in dollars in practical terms. LSE Business Review research published in May 2026 documented that stablecoin transaction volume grew from approximately \$565 billion in 2020 to approximately \$11 trillion in 2025, an annual increase of roughly 80%, and that stablecoin volume had reached approximately 65% of Visa's total network scale.

This growth is extending the dollar's monetary reach into economies that traditional banking infrastructure does not serve well. North America is a net exporter of stablecoin value to Latin America, Africa, and the Middle East, regions where demand for dollar stability is highest and where local currencies have faced significant pressure. Privately issued dollar stablecoins are effectively exporting the dollar's monetary power through digital rails that operate outside traditional correspondent banking.

The macroeconomic benefit for the United States is substantial. \$3 trillion in dollar backed stablecoins, a figure some forecasters anticipate by the end of the decade, backed by short term Treasuries would represent a meaningful and sustained source of demand for US government debt, supporting Treasury issuance and potentially keeping yields lower than they would otherwise be. This matters especially as traditional foreign buyers of Treasuries, including China and Japan, have reduced their purchases.

China launched its own central bank digital currency in 2020 and has begun paying interest on the digital yuan, signaling its intent to use state issued digital currency as a tool of monetary competition. The EU is developing the digital euro. Against that backdrop, the GENIUS Act represents a strategic choice to let private dollar stablecoins serve as the primary vehicle for extending US monetary power globally, rather than building a government issued CBDC.

Wharton Finance's Max Harris, moderating a panel at the Future of Finance Forum in January 2026, put the stakes plainly: "The GENIUS Act is the end of the beginning, not the end of regulation. Implementing the law and making sure regulations evolve as the market changes will be critical in shaping the future of stablecoins and minimizing the risks posed to financial stability."

---

### Risks and Gaps That Remain

The GENIUS Act is landmark legislation and it is not complete. Several risk vectors deserve ongoing attention from clients and observers.

Reserve stress risk: Circle and Tether are now among the largest holders of short term US Treasuries in the world, larger than many sovereign nations. In a period of market stress where redemption demand surges, forced liquidation of reserve assets by stablecoin issuers could amplify volatility in the Treasury market. The Federal Reserve is monitoring this dynamic, but the supervisory framework for managing a large scale stablecoin run is still being developed.

AML and sanctions gaps: Former CFTC Chairman Timothy Massad noted at the Wharton Future of Finance Forum that unlike banks, stablecoin issuers do not have an intermediary layer that actively prevents fraud or ensures sanctions compliance. The government has effectively outsourced that function to the banks, but for nonbank issuers the responsibility sits with the issuer's own compliance team, creating vulnerability at the edges of the system.

Regulatory arbitrage: Multiple federal agencies (OCC, FDIC, Federal Reserve, FinCEN, OFAC) and state regulators all have overlapping jurisdiction over different issuer types. The interaction between federal and state regimes creates potential for regulatory arbitrage where issuers choose the oversight structure that minimizes compliance burden rather than the one best suited to their risk profile.

Yield loophole politics: The ongoing battle over whether affiliated exchanges can offer yield on regulated stablecoins is far from resolved. If Congress closes the loophole that banking associations are targeting, it would significantly advantage bank issued stablecoins over fintech and crypto native competitors. If the loophole remains, bank deposit flight to higher yielding stablecoin adjacent products becomes a genuine systemic concern over time.

Non dollar competition: Euro stablecoins totaled approximately \$635 million by end of June 2026, driven by Circle's EURC and growing 115% year over year. The Open USD network has concentrated dollar stablecoin infrastructure among major payment incumbents. Singapore and the UAE are developing their own stablecoin ecosystems at key global trade hubs. The dollar's 97% dominance in stablecoins is substantial but not permanent, and the geopolitical momentum toward payment system diversification is real.

---

### Conclusion: What This Means for Your Business

The GENIUS Act is the most consequential financial regulation for the crypto industry since the establishment of the SEC itself. It does not resolve every question, as the path to full implementation runs through multi agency rulemaking that will continue into 2027. But it establishes the ground rules for what is already becoming a multi trillion dollar market.

For businesses operating in payments, treasury management, cross border commerce, or financial infrastructure, the key strategic questions are now clearly defined. Will you issue stablecoins directly or partner with an established issuer? Will you build on regulated stablecoin rails or maintain traditional payment infrastructure? How will you manage the compliance requirements that come with stablecoin integration? And how will you position relative to the intensifying competition between bank and nonbank issuers for the future of digital payments?

The framework is in place. The rulemaking is underway. The effective date is January 18, 2027. The window to prepare is now.
